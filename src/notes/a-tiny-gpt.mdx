A model is a rule with knobs. Training adjusts those knobs using the slopes of its mistakes. Letters become lists of numbers, and attention lets each letter read the letters before it. Now we put those pieces together and let the model read Shakespeare.

This is the phone keyboard from part 2, with a longer memory and a different reading list. It guesses one letter at a time. It has no instructions about characters, speeches, or spelling. All it gets is text and the task of guessing what comes next.

## A handful of passages

Each turn, the trainer picks a handful of short passages from the text. That handful is a batch. Reading `to be`, the model sees `t` and guesses `o`, sees `to` and guesses the space, and so on. It scores all those guesses together. The rule from part 3 keeps each position from seeing its answer, even while the computer works on many positions at once.

The trainer averages the model's surprise over the batch, works out every knob's slope, and adjusts the knobs. That whole turn is one step. This trainer uses Adam, which remembers recent slopes and their sizes. A turn need not lower the loss immediately. The aim is to improve over many turns.

## Practice and an exam

We keep a tenth of the text out of training. Think of practice cards and an exam with different questions. A model that only remembers the practice cards may do badly on the exam.

The model's average surprise on practice text is its training loss. Its average surprise on the text it never trains on is its validation loss. Lower means it gave the actual next letters higher chances on average.

The chart calls these lines practice and exam. Both use the same fixed sets of passages at each check, so the comparisons are fair. The horizontal axis counts training steps. A slow phone takes longer to reach a point, but the point still means the same amount of training.

If the practice line keeps falling while the exam line flattens or rises, the model is learning details that do not carry over. That is overfitting. A single bump is not enough to diagnose it. Look for the lines pulling apart over several checks.

## Write with the current knobs

Writing does not train the model. It guesses a letter, picks one using the loaded die from part 2, then feeds that letter back in to guess again. The numbers stay fixed throughout. Its memory holds at most the last 64 letters.

Early samples are mostly noise. Later ones gain familiar letter groups and some real words. A line that looks like a play can still make no sense. Matching the shape of text does not make a sentence true.

## Keep a saved model

A game lets you load a save file instead of playing from the beginning. A saved set of the model's knobs is a checkpoint. Load the trained one uses the same model after 12,000 offline steps. Its 30,055 numbers occupy 120,220 bytes, plus a small file describing where each number belongs.

The file contains the learned numbers. It does not contain Shakespeare, a list of replies, or the trainer's memory of past slopes. Loading it pauses training and replaces the current model. Train again starts a fresh model so you can watch it learn from noise.

The training and writing happen in a background worker in this tab. Training pauses when the demo leaves the screen or you switch tabs. Leaving the page discards your live run. Nothing you train here is uploaded.

## Look inside the model

The letter map from part 2 and the attention arcs from part 3 give us two views of the learned numbers. The map shows the numbers assigned to each symbol before it reads a sentence. The arcs depend on the sentence as well as those numbers.

This model reads all 39 symbols in the lowercase text, including spaces, line breaks, and punctuation. Each symbol has 32 learned numbers. A screen cannot show all those directions at once. We squeeze the 32 numbers into three directions that preserve as much of their spread as possible. This method is called principal component analysis, or PCA.

The picture loses information. Two symbols can look close here while their full lists differ. The axes are mixtures of the original numbers, not named properties such as vowel or meaning. The directions can change as the model learns, so a turning cloud does not by itself show progress. The exam line is the better check.

## How small is tiny?

Count every learned number in the model and you get its parameter count. These are the knobs training can turn, including the embeddings and the numbers inside each block. More knobs give a model room to learn more patterns, but it also needs enough examples to learn useful settings.

Our total is 12,000 steps times 16 passages times 64 next-letter guesses. It excludes the checks and generated samples, which do not update the knobs. A shorter run in your tab has processed fewer targets. The saved weights use four bytes per parameter, so 30,055 times 4 gives the 120,220 bytes mentioned above.

GPT-2's 40 GB is the size of its collected text, not a count of training tokens. The paper does not give the total number of tokens processed during training, so the table leaves it unknown. GPT-3 does report that total. Its reading list contains about 499 billion tokens, obtained by adding the dataset sizes in [table 2.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.14165#page=9). It trains on 300 billion draws from that mixture, repeating some sources more often than others. Dataset size and training tokens are different quantities.

Our tokens are single characters. GPT-2 and GPT-3 use pieces of words, so their token counts do not measure the same amount of text per token. The comparison shows the difference in size, not a fair contest of their losses. Increasing the model and its training together is what people mean here by scale.

## What we left out

Training on a large reading list to predict the next piece of text gives a model that can continue text. That first broad training is pretraining. Our Shakespeare run is a small example of it. It learns the shape of plays, not how to hold a helpful conversation.

You can then take an existing model and train its knobs further on a more focused set of examples. That is fine-tuning. One route to an assistant trains on example conversations, then uses people's rankings of different replies to guide more training. The [InstructGPT paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155) describes this route. It is a concrete example of what separates a text continuation model from a system that tries to follow instructions.

An assistant such as ChatGPT has far more knobs and learns from far more text than this demo. Training on conversations and feedback also changes which replies it tends to choose. None of those steps makes an answer true. A model can learn to produce a convincing mistake. The papers in the table describe published models, not the undisclosed size or training budget of today's ChatGPT.

We also left out the engineering that makes large models practical. They split work across specialized hardware. They reuse calculations while writing. Their tokenizers handle more languages and symbols than this small alphabet. Choosing and checking the training text takes work too. A model can repeat errors and prejudices from what it reads.

Those omissions matter, but you have now run the learning loop itself. A passage supplies the next-letter answers. The model turns its current knobs into guesses, measures its surprise, and works backward to find slopes. Training adjusts the knobs and tries again. Writing uses the resulting guesses without adjusting them. The phone keyboard from part 2 now has a reading list, a longer context, and attention. You can inspect the numbers it learned in your own tab.
