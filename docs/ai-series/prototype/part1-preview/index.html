how a machine learns

guess the fare

a taxi ride costs a starting fee plus a price for every kilometer. if the fee is 3 dollars and a kilometer costs 2, a 5 kilometer ride costs 3 + 5 × 2 = 13 dollars.

now turn it around. you have eight old rides from another city. you know how far each one went and what you paid, but nobody tells you the fee or the price. can you find a fee and a price that guess these fares well?

the chart has your eight rides. the line is your guess. move the two sliders until the line runs close to every ride. each square shows how far off the guess is for one ride, so a bad guess has big squares. no line touches every ride, because some of them sat in traffic. the number under the chart, next to the word loss, is the average area of the squares. smaller is better.

that is all learning means here. there is a rule with a few knobs, and the machine looks for the knob settings that make its guesses close to what really happened.

the rule you just tuned is a model. it takes a distance and gives back a fare. the two sliders are its parameters, the knobs you turn to make it fit. the price per km is called a weight, because it scales the input. a longer ride gets more of it. the starting fee is called a bias, because it moves every guess up or down by the same amount. the fare the line gives for one ride is a prediction.

that average area is the loss, one number for how far all the guesses are from what the riders really paid. the machine's whole job is to make it small. however you set the sliders, it never gets below , because no line touches every ride.

it uses squares for two reasons. a miss of 4 then counts four times as much as a miss of 2, so big misses matter most. and an area is never negative, so a guess that is too high can't cancel one that is too low.

downhill in fog

you could see the chart while you moved the sliders. the machine has to find the settings another way.

every setting of the two knobs has a loss. lay all the settings out as a floor, the starting fee one way and the price per km the other, and raise each spot by its loss. you get a valley, and the best settings sit at the bottom. the picture squeezes the tall parts so the whole valley fits. the cube stands at your current settings, the same ones the sliders set, and the small chart shows the line they make.

the machine never sees this picture. two knobs can be drawn, and this fare rule even has a shortcut formula that finds its bottom. but a real model has millions of knobs. nobody can draw a valley with millions of directions, and a network's valley is usually lumpier, with no formula for its bottom. walking downhill still works there, though it may stop in a low spot that isn't the lowest. so it is like standing on a hill in thick fog. you can't see the bottom. you can only feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, so you take a step that way and feel again. turn on the fog to see only what the machine can feel. you can also drag the valley to turn it, or click a spot to put the cube there.

press step a few times. each step turns both knobs a little, in the direction where the ground drops fastest. the slope under your feet is called the gradient. each step moves every knob downhill by its slope times a number called the learning rate, so steeper ground or a bigger learning rate means a bigger step. walking downhill one step at a time is gradient descent, and walking until the loss stops getting much smaller is training.

the step size matters. crawl takes tiny steps and needs hundreds of them. good walks down the wall and then along the floor of the valley. zigzag overshoots and lands on the far wall, again and again, so it wastes steps. too big jumps across the valley and lands higher than it started. from where the cube starts, it does that on every step, so the loss grows instead of shrinking. for these eight rides, any step size above about climbs out of the valley sooner or later, even when the first few steps look fine, unless it starts right at the bottom.

once the cube stops, ask the line about a ride it has never seen. none of the eight rides went 1.6 km. right now the line has a starting fee of dollars and a price per km of , so for 1.6 km it says + 1.6 × , which is about dollars. using a trained model to answer a new question like this is inference.

which way is downhill?

each step needs the slope for every knob. here is how the machine gets it. take one ride and work out its loss like a receipt, one line at a time. distance times price per km. plus the starting fee, which gives the guessed fare. minus what the rider paid, which gives the miss. then the miss times itself, which is this ride's loss.

press nudge to add one cent to the price per km and watch the bottom line. it changes a little. that change divided by one cent is very close to the slope for this knob. it says how fast the loss moves when the knob turns, and which way. a negative slope means turning the knob up makes the loss smaller.

nudging works, but a real model has millions of knobs, and nudging each one in turn would take forever. so the machine walks the receipt from the bottom line up instead, like tracing a wrong total back to the items that caused it. a line's slope says how much the bottom line moves for each unit that line moves, and each line works it out from the line after it.

the bottom line is the miss times itself. if the miss is 3, this ride's loss is 9. make the miss 3.01 and the loss is 9.0601, about 0.06 more, which is 2 × 3 × 0.01. so the loss moves about two times the miss for each unit the miss moves, and the miss gets a slope of two times the miss. the guessed fare moves the miss one for one, so it gets the same slope, and so does the starting fee. the price per km moves its line only times as much, because the ride was km, so its slope is times that. press backward to watch it. the slope it gets for the price per km is almost exactly what the nudge gave, and the starting fee got its slope in the same walk. this walk backwards is backpropagation. do it for all eight rides, average the slopes, and you have the gradient from the valley.

when a straight line isn't enough

a new question. is a day comfortable indoors? each point is a day, temperature across and humidity up, filled if people said comfortable and hollow if not. the comfortable days sit in the middle. not too cold, not too hot, not too dry, not too damp.

take the taxi rule and give it two inputs, temperature and humidity, each with its own weight, plus a bias. then squash the result so big sums level off. that is one neuron. it draws one straight fence across the chart and says how far onto the yes side a day is. one fence can't pen in the middle. wherever it runs, the side that holds all the comfortable days holds some uncomfortable ones too.

so put several neurons side by side. that is a layer, and it draws several fences. each neuron hands its number to every neuron in the next layer, which gives each one a weight, adds its bias, and squashes again. that is how a second layer reads those fences and joins them into a shape, and four fences make a box around the comfortable days. the last neuron squashes its answer to between 0 for no and 1 for yes. the squash is called the activation function. without it, stacking fences only ever makes another straight fence. layers in a row are a network.

press train and watch the shape form. this network has two layers of eight neurons and one more neuron that gives the answer. for a yes or no answer, it scores its guesses a little differently from the squares, and part 2 explains how. its steps also remember the last few slopes, a common trick called adam, so it gets there faster.

the shade behind the points is its answer for every spot, stronger where it is surer the day is comfortable. each neuron in the first layer gets a row of three squares, one for each input and one for its bias. a filled square is a positive number and a hollow one is a negative number. a bigger square is a number farther from zero. the layers after it decide what those numbers do to the answer. point at a row to draw that neuron's fence.
